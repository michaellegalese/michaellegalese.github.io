1f:T3189,<h1>How obligations unfold</h1>
<p>A regulative rule says who must, may or must not do what, by when, and what follows. This page explains how such a rule behaves over time: what it waits for, how it moves forward, and what it can end in. For the keywords themselves, see the reference: <a href="/l4/reference/regulative">Regulative rules</a>, <a href="/l4/reference/regulative/PARTY">PARTY, MUST, MAY, SHANT</a> and <a href="/l4/reference/regulative/consequences">Deadlines and consequences</a>.</p>
<p>Regulative rules sit beside constitutive rules, which define what things are. A constitutive rule answers "is the borrower a small business?". A regulative rule answers "what does the borrower have to do now, given everything that has happened so far?". <a href="/l4/concepts/legal-modeling/constitutive-vs-regulative">Definitions and duties</a> explains why L4 keeps the two apart.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="a-contract-awaiting-events">A contract awaiting events</h2>
<p>This is the most important idea in the regulative part of L4, and the one most often missed:</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>A regulative rule decides nothing by itself. It is a contract that waits until it is given a start time and a sequence of events.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<p>A <code>DEONTIC</code> value behaves like a function of two inputs:</p>
<pre><code>contract  ×  (start time, events)  →  verdict
</code></pre>
<ul>
<li>The <strong>start time</strong> places "now" on a timeline. Deadlines and event times are measured on the same timeline.</li>
<li>The <strong>events</strong> are what actually happened, in order. Each records who acted, what they did, and when.</li>
<li>The <strong>verdict</strong> is <code>FULFILLED</code>, a breach, or a <em>residual obligation</em>: the contract, partly advanced and still waiting.</li>
</ul>
<p>So a regulative rule is more than a yes-or-no check. It watches a timeline, moves forward as events arrive, and keeps enough state to be resumed later.</p>
<p><strong>Example:</strong></p>
<pre><code class="language-l4-file">-- How a regulative rule plays out against events.
-- Times are numbers on one timeline; read them as days.

DECLARE Party IS ONE OF
    `the seller`
    `the buyer`

DECLARE Action IS ONE OF
    `deliver the goods`
    `pay the invoice`

GIVETH A DEONTIC Party Action
`the delivery obligation` MEANS
    PARTY  `the seller`
    MUST   `deliver the goods`
    WITHIN 14
    HENCE  FULFILLED
    LEST   BREACH BY `the seller` BECAUSE "did not deliver within 14 days"

-- 1. The seller delivers on day 10: FULFILLED
#TRACE `the delivery obligation` AT 0 WITH
    PARTY `the seller` DOES `deliver the goods` AT 10

-- 2. The next event comes on day 20, after the deadline: BREACH BY `the seller`
#TRACE `the delivery obligation` AT 0 WITH
    PARTY `the seller` DOES `deliver the goods` AT 20

-- 3. The wrong party acts on day 10: the event does not match and is skipped.
--    The result is a residual obligation: the seller must still deliver, WITHIN 4.
#TRACE `the delivery obligation` AT 0 WITH
    PARTY `the buyer` DOES `deliver the goods` AT 10

-- 4. No events at all: the result is the obligation itself, still waiting.
#TRACE `the delivery obligation` AT 0 WITH
</code></pre>
<p>The four <code>#TRACE</code> runs in the example give four different results from one rule:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Events</th>
<th>Result</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>The seller delivers on day 10</td>
<td><code>FULFILLED</code></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>The next event is on day 20</td>
<td><code>BREACH BY</code> the seller, with the reason given in <code>LEST</code></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>The buyer "delivers" on day 10</td>
<td>A residual obligation: the seller must deliver within 4</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>No events</td>
<td>A residual obligation: the rule itself, still waiting</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>The last two rows surprise most people. A missing event is not a breach. Nothing has happened yet that shows the deadline passed, so the contract is still open.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="events-and-the-timeline">Events and the timeline</h2>
<p>An event is a triple:</p>
<pre><code>(party, action, time)
</code></pre>
<p>The contract reads events in order. With each event, two things move: the contract's clock advances to the event's time, and the remaining window of the current <code>WITHIN</code> shrinks by the time that has passed. Then:</p>
<ul>
<li>If the event comes <strong>after the deadline</strong>, the contract takes the branch for a missed deadline, without looking at what the event was.</li>
<li>If the event is <strong>in time and matches</strong> the duty (right party, right action, any <code>PROVIDED</code> condition true), the contract takes the branch for the action being done.</li>
<li>If the event is <strong>in time but does not match</strong>, it is skipped. The next event is tried, with the clock further on.</li>
</ul>
<p>For <code>MUST</code>, doing the action leads to <code>HENCE</code> and a missed deadline to <code>LEST</code>. For <code>MAY</code>, both lead to <code>HENCE</code>, because not using a permission is no breach. A prohibition, <code>SHANT</code>, turns <code>MUST</code> around: doing the forbidden act leads to <code>LEST</code>, and reaching the deadline without it leads to <code>HENCE</code>. In every case, <code>HENCE</code> names the outcome the law wants, and <code>LEST</code> the one it does not.</p>
<p>The timeline is just numbers. It can stand for days, hours or business days, as long as deadlines and event times use the same unit.</p>
<p>A <code>WITHIN</code> window starts when its obligation becomes active. In a chain, the next duty's window starts at the event that fulfilled the one before: if the buyer confirms an order on day 10 and the seller then has <code>WITHIN 5</code>, the seller's deadline is day 15. There is no syntax for counting a deadline from some other named event, and none for a deadline on a fixed date; build the timing by chaining obligations with <code>HENCE</code>.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="three-possible-outcomes">Three possible outcomes</h2>
<p>After a contract has read its events, it is in one of three states.</p>
<p><strong><code>FULFILLED</code>.</strong> Every duty was discharged. Nobody has anything left to do under this contract.</p>
<p><strong>Breach.</strong> A party failed a duty, or did something forbidden, and no fallback took over. The breach records who breached and, if <code>BECAUSE</code> was used, why. That reason is what a reviewer or a downstream system reads, so it is worth writing.</p>
<p><strong>A residual obligation.</strong> The events ran out before the contract was fulfilled or breached. The result is a new <code>DEONTIC</code> value: the contract in its current state, with the clock where the events stopped, any <code>WITHIN</code> window shortened, and any chain ready to resume from its current step. It is a first-class value, so you can:</p>
<ul>
<li>inspect it: what is still owed, by whom, and how much time is left?</li>
<li>store it, to keep a long-running contract between sessions;</li>
<li>give it more events later, to resume where it left off.</li>
</ul>
<p>This is what makes L4 a contract language rather than only a checking language. A contract can be partly performed and picked up again at any time.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="chains-fallbacks-and-repeating-duties">Chains, fallbacks and repeating duties</h2>
<p>Larger contracts are built by putting rules inside rules.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Chains.</strong> <code>HENCE</code> can name another obligation: once the seller delivers, the buyer must pay. One stream of events runs through the whole chain, and each step starts reading where the last one stopped.</li>
<li><strong>Fallbacks.</strong> <code>LEST</code> can name another obligation instead of a breach: pay on time, or pay more within a further period. This is how reparation, penalties and grace periods are written. The contract walks down the fallbacks until something is fulfilled, or a step with no fallback is missed and breaches.</li>
<li><strong>Repeating duties.</strong> A function can return the next period's obligation and call itself in <code>HENCE</code>, so instalments or periodic reports need no fixed-length list of steps.</li>
<li><strong>Parallel duties.</strong> <code>RAND</code> runs two obligations side by side and needs both; <code>ROR</code> needs either.</li>
</ul>
<p><a href="/l4/reference/regulative/consequences">Deadlines and consequences</a> gives the syntax and the exact rules for each.</p>
<hr>

   ┌────────────────────────────────────┐
   │   FULFILLED  /  BREACH  /  another │
   │  obligation (which becomes the     │
   │  new waiting state — go to top)    │
   └────────────────────────────────────┘
</code></pre>
<p>If the events run out before the loop ends, the contract in progress is returned as a residual obligation.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="running-a-contract">Running a contract</h2>
<p><code>#TRACE</code> runs a contract from a start time against a fixed list of events and reports the verdict, as in the example above. The same thing is available as a function, <code>EVALTRACE</code>, whose type states the mental model directly:</p>
<pre><code>EVALTRACE  :  DEONTIC party action          -- the contract
           →  NUMBER                        -- start time
           →  LIST OF EVENT party action    -- events
           →  DEONTIC party action          -- the result: finished or residual
</code></pre>
<p>To advance a long-running contract step by step, call <code>EVALTRACE</code> again on the residual from the previous call, starting at the time where the previous events stopped. <a href="/l4/reference/regulative/EVENT">EVENT</a> shows how to build the event list.</p>
